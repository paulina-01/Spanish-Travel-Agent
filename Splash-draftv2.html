Special consonants — CH · LL · Ñ
CH
(che)
Sounds like the "ch" in English cheese.
¡Chévere! — "Cool / Awesome"
LL
(elle)
Sounds like the "y" in yes. Regional variation applies.
lluvia — "rain"
Ñ
(eñe)
Sounds like "ny" in canyon. A completely separate letter from N.
niño — "kid / boy"
The tricky trio — H · J · G
H
(silent)
Always completely silent — never pronounced.
hola → "oh-lah", not "hoh-lah"
J
(heh)
A strong rasping "h" — never like English J.
jugo → "juice" (sounds like "hoo-go")
G
(context)
Like J before E/I. Hard G before A/O/U or closed vowel after Ü.
gato → hard G  |  pingüino → hard G

Tonic syllable
"The syllable you stress changes the entire feel of a word — and Spanish gives you a rulebook."
The three stress rules
Rule 1 — ends in vowel / N / S → stress second-to-last syllable
che·ve·re
Rule 2 — accent mark (tilde) → stress that syllable, overrides everything
con·ti·nua·ción
Rule 3 — ends in consonant (not N or S) → stress last syllable
con·ti·nuar
Quick drill
Mark the stressed syllable: pingüino · sandía · árbol · ciudad

Gender + pronunciation drill

Practice drill
Read each word aloud and mark the stressed syllable. Then translate the capstone sentence and name every pronunciation rule you used.
Culture note — Chévere packs the tonic syllable rule, the CH sound, and the accent mark all into one word. It's used across Latin America to mean "cool" — the perfect pronunciation showcase.
